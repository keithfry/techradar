GAMEGO: Training Game-Dev Agents with Synthetic Trajectories Anchored in Real-World Assets

GameGo is a framework designed to train game-development agents using synthetic trajectories anchored in real-world assets. It transforms brief game seeds into comprehensive Product Requirements Documents by incorporating industry development practices. The method uses task-specific dynamic compression to maintain information density while preserving instruction following during design exploration. This process generates GameGoData, comprising development trajectories for 2D, 2.5D, and 3D games, along with a benchmark. Training on this data results in a model that performs comparably to frontier models across gamedev benchmarks.

FluidPD: In-Place Elasticity for SLO-Aware Prefill-Decode Disaggregated LLM Serving

Prefill-decode disaggregation for LLM serving is challenged by dynamic prefill-to-decode demand ratios, leading to SLO violations despite available capacity. FluidPD introduces in-place elasticity through FluidToken, which handles transient imbalance by offloading prefill to decode workers. FluidRole manages sustained imbalance by dynamically reassigning workers between prefill and decode roles without reloading. These mechanisms use lightweight pressure indices to gauge resource pressure before SLO breaches occur. FluidPD improved SLO attainment by up to 94.6 percentage points compared to static SGLang.

Anchor Divergence for Semantic Geometry in Contrastive Learning

Contrastive representations can encompass a family of geometries beyond simple cosine similarity to capture context-dependent semantic similarity. The paper proposes using contrastive learning, exponential families, and information geometry to link probability distributions over anchors to Bregman geometries. This correspondence is used to define "Anchor Divergences," which specify context-specific semantic geometries on fixed representations. Modeling the anchor distribution under this framework allows for specifying context-specific semantic similarity efficiently. Experiments demonstrate that anchor divergences provide an effective method for specifying semantic similarity in retrieval tasks.

Metonymic Circuits for Abstract Concept Grounding in Vision Transformers

The study investigates how Vision Transformers ground abstract concepts like "angry" when direct referential evidence is scarce. The research hypothesizes a metonymic grounding mechanism where abstract predictions are driven by concrete anchor concepts, such as "fire," bridging visual signals to semantics. Applying Transcoders on vision encoders reveals intermediate features associated with concrete concepts that contribute to abstract concept recognition circuits. Experiments on icon datasets demonstrated structured circuits where perceptual primitives and object-like anchors precede abstract targets. Causal interventions confirmed that these metonymic intermediates are functionally involved in grounding abstract concepts.

When Does External Guidance Help LLM Reasoning? A Bias-Variance Theory of Guidance-Augmented GRPO

Guidance-Augmented GRPO is a theoretical framework that treats external guidance as a stochastic operator to rewrite the question distribution in reinforcement learning. This framework analyzes the policy-gradient estimator as a biased on-policy estimator, bounding its bias by the total-variation guidance divergence. It proves that GA-GRPO converges to a neighborhood of the GRPO stationary point at a rate of $O(1/\sqrt{T})$. The optimal guidance weight is derived as a function of time and divergence, and a minimax lower bound for the unavoidable bias term is established. Experiments on math benchmarks confirm that the optimal-weight GA-GRPO method outperforms existing guidance methods and reduces computational time.

The research proposes using a neutrosophic ensemble classification method for uncertainty-aware bearing fault detection, moving beyond scalar confidence scores. Neural operators for solving PDEs present a refinement-allocation problem due to varying prediction errors across space and time. Memory Prediction Excess: A Probabilistic Quantity for Predictive Gain and Memory Length in Stochastic Processes

Memory Prediction Excess (MPE) quantifies the average improvement in prediction accuracy gained by using the full observed history over using only the static marginal distribution in discrete-time processes. This quantity measures the difference between the expected optimal conditional prediction accuracy and the optimal static prediction accuracy. The framework introduces a normalized version as a dimensionless measure of predictive efficiency and examines its properties. A finite-history MPE (FH-MPE) is defined to measure the predictive gain from retaining only the most recent observations. The research connects MPE to the minimal memory length required to achieve a certain predictive performance.

The research proposes two unsupervised sparse tensor decomposition methods for anomaly detection in multivariate functional data. Entrywise Sparse CP Decomposition (ES-CP) uses an $\ell_1$ penalty for localized anomalies, while Fiberwise Sparse-Group Lasso CP Decomposition (FG-Lasso) incorporates both entrywise and fiberwise penalties. These methods represent nominal process behavior via low-rank CP decomposition estimated through alternating optimization. Simulation studies and case studies show that FG-Lasso outperforms other methods in detecting anomalies and localizing faults. The results suggest that matching the sparse penalty to the anticipated fault structure improves detection performance in high-dimensional functional processes.

The research addresses errors-in-variables problems where a target study has a single error-prone surrogate for an unobserved exposure, and external studies provide repeated measurements from different populations. How Inefficient Is Natural Gradient Descent? From Exact Optimality to \Theta ( \sqrt{ \log d } ) Divergence

Natural gradient descent's efficiency is analyzed by comparing it to the optimal Fisher-Rao path for dually flat families. The inefficiency is quantified by the ratio $R$, which measures the Fisher length of the mixture geodesic over the Fisher-Rao distance. For families with quadratic potentials, $R=1$ exactly, while for non-quadratic families, the inefficiency is bounded or grows as $\Theta(\sqrt{\log d})$ in specific regimes. This inefficiency translates to NGD requiring asymptotically $R$ times more steps than an optimizer following the Fisher-Rao geodesic. Experiments confirm these bounds across different families, ranging from exact optimality to $\Theta(\sqrt{\log d})$ divergence.
