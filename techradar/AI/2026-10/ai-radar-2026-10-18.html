What Should an Agent Remember?

Agent memory allows an agent to use information from past work to inform later decisions, such as storing preferences or failed attempts. Effective memory requires a system to retain and make this information accessible across different sessions. A key challenge is determining which past experiences are relevant and how they should be utilized in the present. Frameworks like personalization, self-improvement, and organizational memory help structure the design of agent memory. Ultimately, exploring agent memory involves understanding how experience leads to learning and adaptation.

Anthropic's AI Claude solved a 25-year-old cryptography mystery related to NSA-supplied seeds for the ECDSA standard. These seeds, used to generate elliptic-curve parameters, were a source of suspicion regarding potential backdoors. Claude determined the seeds came from the "lrand48" random number generator initialized with a Unix timestamp between May 1997 and April 1998. The pattern was found because "lrand48" only outputs 31 bits, and the top bits in some seeds were systematically zero, suggesting deliberate backdoors were less likely. Anthropic is donating the associated bounty to the Internet Security Research Group.

The Number Your Quantization Gate Doesn't Have

The article audits acceptance gates in quantization processes, finding that current documentation lacks measurable failure metrics. The core finding is that existing gates, which rely only on accuracy-based comparisons, are structurally incapable of detecting specific, measurable failures during quantization. The author measures a failure in a face verification task, demonstrating that applying an inherited threshold to quantized models results in significantly worse false-accept and false-reject rates compared to the reference. Ultimately, the author suggests that shipping a quantized build requires reporting these error rates at the inherited and new thresholds to ensure accountability.

Both Charts Are Right. So Why Do They Disagree?

Simpson's paradox illustrates how an overall result can contradict the results from subgroup analyses due to differences in group composition. In an example comparing two medical treatments, Treatment A performed better for specific patient types but Treatment B had a higher overall success rate. This discrepancy arises because the overall result is a weighted average, depending on how the groups are distributed. The paradox shows that a summary statistic can hide crucial differences between subgroups without any mathematical error. Therefore, analysts must investigate the context, group comparability, and the specific question being asked when interpreting data.

Deconfusing Corrigibility

Corrigibility is complex because existing definitions are often over-epistemicized or evaluatively-thickened. The author proposes that the fundamental concept underlying corrigibility is respect for authority, using the analogy of a healthcare proxy. This concept dictates that an AI should first respect human authority and refrain from overstepping, and then act on the principal's behalf as they would judge best.
